The idea

Reweight the jumps, recover the rates

A lattice walker that starts in one deep basin waits a long time before it crosses to the other, so direct kinetic Monte Carlo spends nearly all its effort on excursions that fall back. Importance sampling tilts each jump out of a grid state toward the product and accounts for the tilt exactly.

01

The setting

Rare hops on a grid

  • Microstates on a grid
  • Nearest-neighbor jumps
  • Detailed balance
  • Two basins, two saddles

The kinetics

Each point \(i\) of a regular grid \(\Omega\) is a microstate with energy \(E(i)\). The system jumps between nearest neighbors at rates that obey detailed balance with respect to \(e^{-E/k_{\rm B}T}\):

Jump ratesModel

\[ \begin{gathered} r(i \to j) = \nu_0 \exp\!\left[-\frac{E(j)-E(i)}{2k_{\rm B}T}\right] \mathbb{1}_{\rm nn}(i,j), \\ r(i \to j)\, e^{-E(i)/k_{\rm B}T} = r(j \to i)\, e^{-E(j)/k_{\rm B}T}. \end{gathered} \]

Here \(k_{\rm B}T\) is the thermal energy and \(\mathbb{1}_{\rm nn}(i,j)\) is 1 for nearest neighbors and 0 otherwise. On a two-dimensional grid, \(\nu_0 = \mu k_{\rm B}T/(\Delta x)^2\) for mobility \(\mu\) and grid spacing \(\Delta x\), a scaling chosen so that the transition rate between the basins converges as \(\Delta x \to 0\).

Why transitions are rare

Deep basins around the minima A and B are joined through saddle points S1 and S2. At low temperature a walker near A lingers in its basin; a rare crossing passes near S1 or S2. The aim is faster crossings with accurate rates and correct relative path probabilities, which carry the mechanism.

02

Generalized formulation

Boundaries that survive refinement

  • Auxiliary states F and S
  • Finite regions, fixed size
  • Flux kept under refinement
  • Detailed balance kept

Why not single points

Declaring failure and success at the grid points A and B imposes point boundary conditions on the optimal importance function, whose gradients around them grow ever sharper as the grid is refined, like a boundary condition at a single point of a partial differential equation.

Two extra states

Instead, the state space becomes \(\tilde\Omega = \Omega \cup \{{\rm F}, {\rm S}\}\): states in small regions around A and B, fixed in size under refinement, may jump into F or S. A control function \(f(i)\), for example a truncated Gaussian, weights the coupling to F:

Coupling to FDesign choice

\[ \begin{gathered} \tilde r(i \to {\rm F}) = \nu_0 \exp\!\left[-\frac{E({\rm F})-E(i)}{2k_{\rm B}T}\right] f(i), \\ \begin{aligned} f(i) &= \epsilon\,(\Delta x)^d \exp\!\left(-\frac{\lVert \mathbf{x}(i)-\mathbf{x}({\rm A})\rVert^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \\ &\qquad \times \mathbb{1}\big(\lVert \mathbf{x}(i)-\mathbf{x}({\rm A})\rVert < r_{\rm c}\big), \end{aligned} \end{gathered} \]

and likewise \(s(i)\) around B, where \(\mathbf{x}(i)\) is the position of \(i\) in \(d\) dimensions and \(\epsilon\), \(\sigma\), \(r_{\rm c}\) set strength, width and range. The factor \((\Delta x)^d\) keeps the flux between \(i\) and F nearly constant as \(\Delta x\) changes. With energies \(E({\rm F})\) and \(E({\rm S})\) assigned, the extended rates \(\tilde r\) still obey detailed balance.

Paths

A path leaves F and ends at F (a failure) or at S (a success); the walker returns to F many times before it reaches S.

03

Importance sampling

Reweight the jumps, or add a bias

  • Reweighted jump chain
  • Jumps into F removed
  • Optimum: the committor
  • Bias potential

Reweighting

On leaving \(i\), the walker jumps to \(j\) with probability \(\tilde K(i \to j) = \tilde r(i \to j)/\tilde r_{\rm tot}(i)\), where \(\tilde r_{\rm tot}(i)\) is the total rate out of \(i\) and \(j\) ranges over \(\mathcal{A}(i)\): the neighbors of \(i\), F and S. As in earlier importance sampling of rare transitions (Cai et al., 2002; de Koning et al., 2005), an importance function \(I > 0\) with \(I({\rm S}) = 1\) reweights these jumps:

Reweighted jumpsDefinition

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde K'(i \to j) &= \frac{\tilde K(i \to j)\, I(j)}{\tilde n'(i)\, I(i)}, \qquad j \neq {\rm F}, \\ \tilde n'(i) &= \sum_{j \in \mathcal{A}(i) \setminus \{\rm F\}} \tilde K(i \to j)\, \frac{I(j)}{I(i)}. \end{aligned} \]

Jumps into F are removed and \(\tilde n'(i)\) restores normalization; the jump out of F stays unbiased, so \(I({\rm F})\) is never needed.

The optimum

The optimal importance function needs no renormalization, \(\tilde n'(i) = 1\), at every \(i \in \Omega\):

OptimalityExact

\[ \sum_{j \in \mathcal{A}(i) \setminus \{\rm F\}} \tilde K(i \to j)\, I^{\rm opt}(j) = I^{\rm opt}(i). \]

It is the right eigenvector, with eigenvalue 1, of the jump matrix without jumps into F, and equals the discrete committor \(p_{\rm S}(i)\), the probability of reaching S before F. Every success path from a given first state then gains the same known factor, which preserves the mechanism. Finding it exactly is at least as hard as the original problem, so rare-event sampling becomes an optimization problem.

A bias potential

Because each rate carries the factor \(e^{-[E(j)-E(i)]/2k_{\rm B}T}\), reweighting by \(I(j)/I(i)\) is exactly the kinetics on \(E + E_{\rm b}\) for jumps among grid states and into S, with

Equivalent biasExact

\[ E_{\rm b}(i) = -2\, k_{\rm B}T \ln I(i), \]

so finding the optimal importance function is finding the optimal bias potential.

04

Estimator

Unbiased weights, an honest clock

  • Unbiased for any positive importance function
  • Clock from unbiased runs
  • Rare-event limit

Path weights

For a success path \(\Gamma = \{{\rm F}, i_1, \dots, i_n, {\rm S}\}\), the ratios of \(I\) telescope and the normalization factors multiply into a path weight \(W\), linking the probability \(P'\) under \(\tilde K'\) to the original \(P\):

Path identityExact

\[ \begin{aligned} P(\Gamma) &= W(\Gamma)\, I(i_1)\, P'(\Gamma), \\ W(\Gamma) &= \prod_{k=1}^{n} \tilde n'(i_k). \end{aligned} \]

With a reward \(R(\Gamma)\), 1 for a success and 0 otherwise, the probability \(p_{\rm S}({\rm F})\) that a path leaving F reaches S before F becomes an average over biased paths:

Success probabilityExact, any positive \(I\)

\[ p_{\rm S}({\rm F}) = \big\langle R(\Gamma)\, W(\Gamma)\, I(i_1) \big\rangle'. \]

This holds for any positive \(I\), so the estimate is unbiased in expectation: a poor \(I\) costs variance, not bias. For the exact committor, \(p_{\rm S}({\rm F}) = \sum_j \tilde K({\rm F} \to j)\, I^{\rm opt}(j)\) without sampling any transition paths.

A rate without a biased clock

A first passage from F to S is \(N_{\rm F}\) failures and one success, so, with time spent in F excluded,

RateApproximate

\[ \begin{aligned} \langle T_{\rm FS} \rangle &= \langle N_{\rm F} \rangle \langle t_{\rm FF} \rangle + \langle t_{\rm FS} \rangle \\ &\approx \frac{\langle t_{\rm FF} \rangle}{p_{\rm S}({\rm F})}, \\ r_{\rm FS} = \frac{1}{\langle T_{\rm FS} \rangle} &\approx \frac{p_{\rm S}({\rm F})}{\langle t_{\rm FF} \rangle}, \end{aligned} \]

where \(\langle t_{\rm FF} \rangle\) and \(\langle t_{\rm FS} \rangle\) are mean failure and success durations. The first line is exact; the approximations need \(\langle N_{\rm F} \rangle \gg 1\), failures vastly outnumbering successes. Unbiased runs give \(\langle t_{\rm FF} \rangle\); biased runs supply only the dimensionless \(p_{\rm S}({\rm F})\), so time is never measured under bias. Equating \(r_{\rm FS}\) with the rate between the basins assumes that F and S barely perturb the kinetics, which the paper argues holds on fine grids.

In high dimensions: the first jump

Every path starts with an unbiased jump out of F, whose targets cannot be enumerated in high dimensions.

Redefining \(f(i)\) with the factor \(e^{-[E(i)-E({\rm F})]/2k_{\rm B}T}\) and the Boltzmann factor of a convex confinement potential \(E_{\rm C}\) with its minimum at A, for example \(E_{\rm C}(i) = \lVert \mathbf{x}(i)-\mathbf{x}({\rm A})\rVert^2/2\eta^2\), turns that jump into an equilibrium distribution, which standard Monte Carlo can sample even in high dimensions:

Jump out of FDesign choice

\[ \tilde r({\rm F} \to i) \propto \exp\!\left[-\frac{E(i) + E_{\rm C}(i) - E({\rm F})}{k_{\rm B}T}\right] \]

for \(\lVert \mathbf{x}(i)-\mathbf{x}({\rm A})\rVert < r_{\rm c}\). Detailed balance fixes the reverse rate, and the total rate out of F is never needed, since time in F is excluded.

05

Variance control

Keep the weights in a window

  • Branching random walk
  • Russian roulette and splitting
  • Stochastic rounding
  • Unbiased in expectation

Why branch

The estimate is unbiased for any positive \(I\), but the number of paths it needs depends strongly on the quality of \(I\), and far from the committor it becomes excessive.

The walk

A branching random walk starts one walker at F with \(W = 1\), moves every walker with \(\tilde K'\) and multiplies its weight by \(\tilde n'\) at each step. A walker whose weight leaves \([W_{\min}, W_{\max}]\) becomes \(\mathcal{R}(W)\) walkers of weight 1, where \(\mathcal{R}(W) = \lfloor W \rfloor + 1\) with probability \(W - \lfloor W \rfloor\) and \(\lfloor W \rfloor\) otherwise: Russian roulette below the window, splitting above it. A removed walker scores zero.

Why it stays unbiased

This stochastic rounding, widely used in Monte Carlo rendering and particle transport (Arvo and Kirk, 1990; Vorba and Křivánek, 2016; Rath et al., 2022), averages to \(W\). A walker that reaches S scores its current \(W\) times \(I(i_1)\), so the total estimates \(\langle R\, W\, I(i_1) \rangle'\) without bias while every weight stays in the window.

06

Machine learning

A neural network in log space

  • Neural-network bias potential
  • Normalization loss
  • Adaptive sampling
  • Simulated annealing

Why a network, and why log space

Refining the grid multiplies the states, drastically so in high dimensions, where \(I\) cannot be optimized state by state. Building on machine-learning work on committors and rates (Li et al., 2022; Yuan et al., 2024; Mitchell and Rotskoff, 2024), a neural network with parameters \(\theta\) represents the bias potential \(E_{\rm b}(i;\theta)\): near F the optimal \(I\) is extremely small and risks underflow, while \(E_{\rm b}\) stays in a manageable range.

The loss

Training drives every normalization factor toward one:

Training lossDesign choice

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}(\theta) &= \sum_{i} \big[\ln \tilde n'(i;\theta)\big]^2, \\ I(i;\theta) &= e^{-E_{\rm b}(i;\theta)/2k_{\rm B}T}. \end{aligned} \]

Each term vanishes exactly when the optimality condition holds at \(i\); the loss is analogous to one by Mitchell and Rotskoff (2024). Since the condition matters only along transition paths, the sum runs over states that the current biased walks visit.

Annealing and representation

Annealing the training temperature down to the target, the authors found, helps avoid numerical instabilities and aids convergence. In its high-dimensional test, the paper writes the bias as a trainable Gaussian, \(A\,e^{-\sum_k a_k (x_k - c_k)^2}\), plus a multilayer perceptron: the Gaussian speeds early exploration, and the perceptron captures non-Gaussian features.

Takeaway

Rare events become an optimization problem

Importance sampling turns rare-event kinetic Monte Carlo into learning a bias potential. The optimal importance function is the committor, so the optimal bias is \(-2k_{\rm B}T\) times its logarithm; a learned bias only approximates it, yet path reweighting keeps the success-probability estimate unbiased, and branching keeps the variance in check.

  • ExactThe bias-potential equivalence, the committor as optimum, and an estimator unbiased for any positive importance function.
  • ApproximateThe rate as success probability over mean failure time, valid when failures vastly outnumber successes.
  • Design choicesThe coupling to F and S, the grid scaling of rates, log-space training with annealing, and the weight window.
